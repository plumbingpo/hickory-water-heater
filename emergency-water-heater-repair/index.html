Warning Signs to Watch For

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • A T&P valve that releases water repeatedly instead of staying closed
  • An electric water heater that keeps tripping its circuit breaker
  • A complete loss of hot water throughout the house
  • Water pooling around the base of the heater or spreading across the floor
  • The smell of gas near the unit, a situation that calls for evacuating and phoning the utility or 911
  • Hot water far hotter than normal, a sign of a runaway thermostat or gas control
  • Black soot or heat discoloration around the burner door or draft hood
  • Violent rumbling or banging from the heater as it runs

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Emergency calls bring together electricity, gas, heat, pressure, and water, which is exactly the mix that makes do-it-yourself work risky. An electric heater sits on a 240-volt circuit that should never be handled while standing in water. A gas heater has fuel piping, a burner, and a vent that can spill combustion gases if something is out of place. The tank itself holds scalding water under pressure, and a discharging relief valve is signaling an unsafe condition that must be fixed at the source, not muted. Blocking the discharge pipe, relighting next to a gas odor, or forcing a rusted connection are all common and dangerous mistakes.

A trained technician brings the right order of operations and the right parts. Our team confirms the heater is isolated, tests for gas leaks with a detector and soap solution instead of relying on a sniff test, checks household water pressure and thermal expansion when a relief valve has been discharging, and inspects the vent for proper draft before a gas unit is restarted. Just as important, we tell you honestly when a heater can be repaired and when it cannot. A leaking seam or rusted-through bottom cannot be fixed, while a failed valve, fitting, or element usually can, and the difference is often a few hundred dollars versus a new heater. You get that answer before any work begins.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Keep the thermostat near 120F to limit scalding and reduce strain on the tank
  • Consider an automatic leak shutoff that stops the cold supply the moment water hits the floor
  • Ask about a drain pan and drain line, especially for heaters in attics, closets, or above finished rooms
  • Check the temperature and pressure relief valve each year so you know it will work when it matters
  • Know where the heater shutoff and the main water valve are, and make sure everyone in the house does too
  • Leave clear space around a gas heater and never store flammable liquids nearby
  • Flush sediment from the tank yearly, since buildup overheats the bottom and speeds corrosion
